## Consequences of Non-compliance (Source: Pt7 p.19)

- Noncompliance with data protection laws and regulations can have severe consequences and varies by jurisdiction and the specific regulations violated. (Source: Pt7 p.19)
  - Can harm reputation, eroding customer trust, decreasing business opportunities, and potentially losing contracts or partnerships.
  - Software licensing requirements can result in the revocation of usage rights and other consequences such as fines.
- **Fines** — regulatory agencies may impose substantial fines. (Source: Pt7 p.19)
- **Sanctions** — legal sanctions such as financial penalties, legal liabilities, reputational damage, and loss of customer trust; enforced by governing bodies, regulatory authorities, or organizations overseeing the specific domain in which the noncompliance occurred. (Source: Pt7 p.19)
- Legal actions from affected individuals or data subjects may lead to costly lawsuits and settlements. (Source: Pt7 p.19)
- Additional regulatory scrutiny: increased audits, investigations, or mandated remediation measures. (Source: Pt7 p.19)

## Impacts of Contractual Non-compliance (Source: Pt7 p.20)

- **Breach of Contract** — contracts often include provisions on data protection, cybersecurity measures, and safeguarding sensitive information. (Source: Pt7 p.20)
- **Termination of Contracts** — clauses allowing termination if the other party fails to protect data or implement sufficient cybersecurity measures. (Source: Pt7 p.20)
- **Indemnification and Liability** — clauses shifting responsibility for losses or legal expenses from cybersecurity incidents onto the noncompliant party, leading to financial burdens and reputational damage. (Source: Pt7 p.20)
- **Noncompliance Penalties** — contracts may stipulate penalties or financial consequences for noncompliance with cybersecurity requirements. (Source: Pt7 p.20)

## Compliance Monitoring (Source: Pt7 p.21)

- Compliance with legal and regulatory requirements, industry standards, and internal policies is ensured through diligent monitoring of an organization's actions, including investigations, audits, and assessments of third parties. (Source: Pt7 p.21)
- **Due Diligence** — the comprehensive assessment and evaluation of an organization's data protection practices and measures. (Source: Pt7 p.21)
- **Attestation and Acknowledgment** — requiring individuals or entities to formally acknowledge their understanding of compliance obligations and commitment to adhere, through signed agreements, policy acknowledgments, and training activities. (Source: Pt7 p.21)
- **Conducted Internally** — self-assessments, internal audits, and reviews. (Source: Pt7 p.21)
- **Conducted Externally** — independent audits, assessments, or regulatory inspections. (Source: Pt7 p.21)
- **Automation** — streamlines monitoring activities, improves accuracy, and enhances the ability to detect noncompliance or anomalies promptly. (Source: Pt7 p.21)

## Due Diligence, Due Care, and Due Process (Source: Pt7 p.22)

- **Due Diligence** — taking reasonable steps to satisfy a legal requirement; secure information system and data. Sub-bullets: the management of due care; formal process; preparation. (Source: Pt7 p.22)
- **Due Care** — mitigation actions taken by an organization to defend against the risks uncovered during due diligence. Sub-bullets: degree of conduct a person of ordinary prudence exercises under the same or similar circumstance; duty of care; careful act. (Source: Pt7 p.22)
- **Due Process** — principle that the organization must respect and safeguard personnel's rights. (Source: Pt7 p.22)

## Privacy (Source: Pt7 p.23)

- **Privacy Data** — personally identifiable or sensitive information associated with an individual's personal, financial, or social identity, including data that, if exposed or mishandled, could infringe upon privacy rights. (Source: Pt7 p.23)
  - Protecting it carries significant local, national, and global legal implications.
  - Many countries have privacy laws dictating how personal data is handled within their jurisdiction; they define individuals' rights, organizations' responsibilities, and procedures for data protection and privacy enforcement.
- **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** (Source: Pt7 p.23):
  - Protects the personal data of European Union (EU) citizens while within EU or EEA (European Economic Area) borders.
  - Personal data cannot be collected, processed, or retained without the individual's informed consent.
  - Sensitive personal data includes religious beliefs, political opinions, trade union membership, gender, sexual orientation, racial or ethnic origin, genetic data, and health information.
  - Gives data subjects rights to withdraw consent, and to inspect, amend, or erase data held about them.

## Privacy Laws (Source: Pt7 p.24)

| Law | Slide description | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **FISMA** — Federal Information Security Management Act | Requires program officials and the head of each agency to conduct annual reviews of information security programs; governs the security of data processed by federal government agencies | Pt7 p.24 |
| **HIPAA** — Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act | Governs the disclosure and protection of health information | Pt7 p.24 |
| **SOX** — Sarbanes-Oxley Act | Governs the disclosure of financial and accounting information; protects privacy and integrity of financial data in publicly traded corporations; protects investors from financial fraud and holds executives accountable for all financial dealings within their organization | Pt7 p.24 |
| **GLBA** — Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act | For financial services; enables banks to consolidate to protect confidentiality and integrity of personal financial information stored by financial institutions; removed previous legal barriers to allow banks, insurers, and security companies to merge; institutes new standards on how consumer information is shared and what is disclosed to the consumer | Pt7 p.24 |

## Data Subject / Ownership (Source: Pt7 p.25)

- Organizations that collect and process personal data are considered **custodians or stewards** of the data rather than owners. Under GDPR the emphasis is on the rights and protections of the data subject rather than determining ownership. (Source: Pt7 p.25)
- **Data Subject** — an individual identified by privacy data; an individual whose personal data is processed by an organization or other entity; has control over their personal data and can exercise rights such as the right to access, rectify, and delete their data. (Source: Pt7 p.25)
- **Data Owner** — a senior (executive) role with ultimate responsibility for maintaining the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of an information asset. (Source: Pt7 p.25)

## Data Controller vs. Data Processor (Source: Pt7 p.26)

| | Data Controller | Data Processor |
|---|---|---|
| Core definition | Entity that determines the **purposes and means** of processing personal data | Processes personal data **on behalf of** the Data Controller |
| Decision power | Overall control and responsibility; decides why and how data is processed; defines purposes and categories of data | No independent decision-making power; acts under the authority and instructions of the controller; processes data solely as instructed |
| Obligations | Direct legal obligations under data protection laws; accountable for compliance, obtaining consent from data subjects, providing privacy notices, implementing data protection policies and procedures, handling data subject requests | Legal obligation to process personal data only for the purposes defined by the controller |

(Source: Pt7 p.26)

## Data Inventory and Retention (Source: Pt7 p.27)

- **Data Inventory** — list of classified data or information stored or processed by a system. (Source: Pt7 p.27)
  - Helps determine appropriate retention periods for different categories of personal data, ensuring compliance with data storage limitation requirements.
  - Gives an overview of the types of data handled, purposes for processing, the legal basis, and recipients of the data, for transparency and accountability; organizations can document processing activities in compliance with privacy laws.
- **Data Retention** — the process an organization uses to maintain the existence of and control over certain data to comply with business policies and/or applicable laws and regulations. Organizations must retain personal data only as long as necessary to fulfill the intended purpose or as required by law. (Source: Pt7 p.27)

## Right to Be Forgotten (RTBF) (Source: Pt7 p.28)

- **RTBF** — principle of regulated privacy data that protects the data subject's ability to request its deletion. (Source: Pt7 p.28)
  - Discussed and put into practice in several jurisdictions, including Argentina, the European Union (EU), and the Philippines.
  - A fundamental principle in GDPR granting data subjects the right to request erasure or deletion of their personal data under certain circumstances.
  - The right to have private information about a person removed from Internet searches and other directories under some circumstances.
  - Also known as the **right to erasure** (underlined on the slide).
